\subsection{Problem 7. Differentiation rules and linear approximation}
Let
\[f(x)=\frac{e^{2x}}{x^{2}+1}.\]

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Compute $f^{\prime}(x)$ using the quotient rule and the chain rule. Simplify your result as much as reasonably possible.
    \item Find the linear approximation (tangent line approximation) of $f$ at $x=0$ and use it to approximate $f(0.1)$.
    \item Give a numerical estimate of the absolute error between your linear approximation and the exact value of $f(0.1)$ (you may use a calculator for the exponential).
    \item Explain why the linear approximation is expected to be accurate near $x=0$ and why it becomes less reliable as $|x|$ grows. Relate this discussion to the second derivative of $f$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Strategy}
\subsection*{Solution}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]

    \item \textbf{Compute $f'(x)$} \\
    Let $f(x) = \frac{e^{2x}}{x^2+1}$. We identify:
    \begin{align*}
        u &= e^{2x} & v &= x^2+1 \\
        u' &= 2e^{2x} \quad (\text{Chain Rule}) & v' &= 2x
    \end{align*}
    Applying the Quotient Rule:
    \[ f'(x) = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2} = \frac{2e^{2x}(x^2+1) - e^{2x}(2x)}{(x^2+1)^2} \]
    
    Now, we simplify by factoring out $2e^{2x}$ from the numerator:
    \begin{align*}
        f'(x) &= \frac{2e^{2x} \left[ (x^2+1) - x \right]}{(x^2+1)^2} \\
        &= \mathbf{\frac{2e^{2x}(x^2 - x + 1)}{(x^2+1)^2}}
    \end{align*}

    \item \textbf{Linear Approximation at $x=0$} \\
    The formula for linear approximation at $a=0$ is $L(x) = f(0) + f'(0)(x-0)$.
    
    First, find the values at $x=0$:
    \[ f(0) = \frac{e^{2(0)}}{0^2+1} = \frac{1}{1} = 1 \]
    \[ f'(0) = \frac{2e^0(0^2 - 0 + 1)}{(0^2+1)^2} = \frac{2(1)}{1} = 2 \]
    
    The linear approximation is:
    \[ L(x) = 1 + 2(x) = \mathbf{1 + 2x} \]
    
    Approximating $f(0.1)$:
    \[ f(0.1) \approx L(0.1) = 1 + 2(0.1) = \mathbf{1.2} \]

    \item \textbf{Absolute Error Calculation} \\
    First, we calculate the exact value of $f(0.1)$:
    \[ f(0.1) = \frac{e^{0.2}}{(0.1)^2+1} = \frac{e^{0.2}}{1.01} \approx \frac{1.2214027}{1.01} \approx 1.20931 \]
    
    Now, calculate the absolute error:
    \[ \text{Error} = | f(0.1) - L(0.1) | \approx | 1.20931 - 1.2 | = \mathbf{0.00931} \]

    \item \textbf{Accuracy and Second Derivative} \\
    The linear approximation $L(x)$ represents the tangent line to the curve at $x=0$.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Why it is accurate near 0:} At the point of tangency ($x=0$), the line and the curve touch and have the same slope. For very small $\Delta x$, the curve has not had "time" to bend away from the line significantly.
        \item \textbf{Why it breaks down for large $x$:} The function $f(x)$ is curved, while $L(x)$ is straight. As we move away from $x=0$, this curvature causes the gap between the function and the line to widen.
        \item \textbf{Relation to $f''(x)$:} The second derivative $f''(x)$ measures the \textbf{concavity} (or curvature) of the function. The error in linear approximation is approximately proportional to $\frac{1}{2}f''(c)(x-a)^2$ (from Taylor's Theorem). If $f''(x)$ is large, the graph curves sharply, and the linear approximation becomes inaccurate very quickly. If $f''(x) \approx 0$, the graph is nearly flat, and the line remains accurate for longer.
    \end{itemize}

\end{enumerate}
