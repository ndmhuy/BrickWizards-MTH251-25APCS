\subsection{Problem 4. Limits and continuity with a parameter}
Consider the functions:
\[ f(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^{2}-1}{x-1},&x\ne1,\\ a,&x=1,\end{cases} \quad \text{and} \quad g(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sin(bx)}{x},&x\ne0,\\ 1,&x=0,\end{cases} \]
where $a$ and $b$ are real parameters.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow1}\frac{x^{2}-1}{x-1}$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow0}\frac{\sin(bx)}{x}$ (express your answer for the second limit in terms of $b$).
    \item Find the values of $a$ and $b$ such that $f$ is continuous at $x=1$ and $g$ is continuous at $x=0$.
    \item For your chosen values of $a$ and $b$, sketch the graphs of $f$ and $g$ near the problematic points and explain how the value at the point is "patched" to make the function continuous.
    \item Explain why changing only the value at a single point can never make a function differentiable at that point if the two one-sided derivatives from the limit expression disagree.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Strategy}
To solve this problem, we will systematically apply the definitions of continuity and differentiability.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item \textbf{Analyze Limits:} We will first simplify the algebraic expressions for $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ to evaluate their limits as $x$ approaches the points ($x=1$ for $f$ and $x=0$ for $g$). For $g(x)$, we need to handle the parameter $b$ carefully, distinguishing between $b=0$ and $b \neq 0$.
    \item \textbf{Apply Continuity Definition:} We will use the condition $\lim_{x \to c} h(x) = h(c)$ to find the required values for parameters $a$ and $b$.
    \item \textbf{Geometric Interpretation:} We will interpret these results graphically, viewing the continuity condition as filling a "hole" in an otherwise smooth curve.
    \item \textbf{Differentiability Logic:} We will distinguish between "patching a hole" (continuity) and "smoothing a corner" (differentiability) to explain why continuity does not guarantee differentiability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Solution}
\textbf{(a) Computing the limits}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$
    \[ \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2-1}{x-1} = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = \lim_{x \to 1} x+1 = 2 \]
    \item $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(bx)}{x}$ \\
    \textbf{Case 1: $b = 0$}
    \[ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(bx)}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{0}{x} = 0 \]
    \textbf{Case 2: $b \neq 0$}
    \[ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(bx)}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(bx)}{bx/b} \]
    Let $u = bx$. As $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} u = 0$, then:
    \[ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(bx)}{bx/b} = \lim_{u \to 0} \frac{b\sin(u)}{u} = b\lim_{u \to 0} \frac{\sin(u)}{u} = b \]
    (Applying $\displaystyle \lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1$ proved in Appendix \ref{appendix:lim}) \\
    In the end, whether $b = 0$ or $b \neq 0$, we always obtain:
    \[ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(bx)}{x} = b \]
\end{itemize}

\textbf{(b) Finding parameters for continuity}
\begin{itemize}
    \item For $f$ to be continuous at $x = 1$, we need:
    \[ \lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = f(1) \Longleftrightarrow \boxed{a = 2} \]
    \item For $g$ to be continuous at $x = 0$, we need:
    \[ \lim_{x \to 0} g(x) = g(0) \Longleftrightarrow \boxed{b = 1}\]
\end{itemize}

    For $x \neq 1$, the function simplifies to $f(x)=\dfrac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x+1$. This is the equation of a straight line. The expression $\dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ is undefined at $x = 1$, creating a "hole" (removable discontinuity) at $(1,2)$ in the graph of $y=x+1$. \\
    By defining $f(1)=a=2$, we place a point exactly at the hole. This "patches" the graph, filling the hole and making the function continuous. The graph of $f(x)$ is the complete line $y=x+1$
    \item \textbf{Graph of g}

    With $b=1$, $g(x) = \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$. It has a hole at $x=0$ because the expression is undefined there. Defining $g(0)=b=1$ fills this gap, connecting the left and right branches smoothly.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{(d) Continuity vs. Differentiability}
Differentiability at a point $x=c$ requires the existence of the limit of the difference quotient:
\[ f'(c) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{h} \]
For this limit to exist, the left-hand derivative (slope coming from the left) and the right-hand derivative (slope coming from the right) must be equal. \\
Changing the value of $f(c)$ at a single point can repair a \textbf{removable discontinuity} (a hole), making the function continuous. However, it cannot repair a \textbf{geometric corner} or "kink".
\begin{itemize}
    \item If the one-sided derivatives disagree (e.g., slope is $-1$ from the left and $+1$ from the right), the graph has a sharp corner.
    \item No matter what value we assign to the point $f(c)$, the slopes approaching that point remain different. Therefore, no single tangent line exists, and the function remains non-differentiable.
\end{itemize}
