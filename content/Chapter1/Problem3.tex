\subsection{Problem 3. Trigonometric modeling}
The number of hours of daylight $D$ in a certain city can be approximated as a function of the day of the year $t$ (measured in days, with $t=0$ on January 1) by a sinusoidal model:
\[D(t)=A+B \cos(\omega(t-t_{0})).\]
Suppose you are given that the longest day has 14 hours of daylight (around day $t\approx172$) and the shortest day has 10 hours of daylight (around day $t\approx355$).

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Determine $A$ and $B$ from the information about the longest and shortest days. Explain the meaning of these two parameters.
    \item Argue that a reasonable choice for $\omega$ is $\dfrac{2\pi}{365}$. Explain briefly where this comes from.
    \item Choose a suitable value of $t_{0}$ so that the maximum of $D(t)$ occurs around $t=172$. Write the explicit function $D(t)$ with your chosen parameters.
    \item Using your model, estimate the number of hours of daylight on day $t=80$ and discuss qualitatively how accurate you expect the model to be (e.g., weather effects, geographical assumptions).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Strategy}
\subsection*{Solution}

\begin{itemize}

%------------------ (a)
\item[(a)] \textbf{Determining $A$ and $B$.}

Without loss of generality, let $A,B>0$.  
We have $-1 \le \cos\theta \le 1$, so
\[
\begin{cases}
D(t)_{\min} = A - B = 10,\\[4pt]
D(t)_{\max} = A + B = 14.
\end{cases}
\]
Solving:
\[
A = 12, \qquad B = 2.
\]

$A$ is the \emph{average number of hours of daylight}.  
$B$ is the \emph{amplitude of seasonal variation}, meaning daylight deviates by $\pm 2$ hours from the average.

%------------------ (b)
\item[(b)] \textbf{Choosing $\omega$.}

The model should repeat every year, so  
\[
T = 365 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{2\pi}{365}.
\]

%------------------ (c)
\item[(c)] \textbf{Choosing $t_0$ so that the maximum occurs at $t=172$.}

We want
\[
D(172) = 12 + 2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(172 - t_0)\right) = 14,
\]
which requires
\[
\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(172 - t_0)\right) = 1.
\]

Thus,
\[
\frac{2\pi}{365}(172 - t_0) = 2k\pi, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.
\]

Choosing $k=0$ gives
\[
172 - t_0 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad t_0 = 172.
\]

Therefore the model is
\[
D(t) = 12 + 2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(t - 172)\right).
\]

%------------------ (d)
\item[(d)] \textbf{Estimating $D(80)$.}

Plug into the model:
\[
D(80) = 12 + 2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(80 - 172)\right)
       \approx 11.97\ \text{hours}.
\]

\textbf{Accuracy considerations:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The model is simplified.  
    \item It ignores weather and cloud cover.  
    \item It ignores geographic effects.  
    \item It uses a perfect cosine, but real daylight variation is not perfectly sinusoidal.  
    \item Leap years slightly shift the cycle.  
\end{itemize}

Still, for general estimation, the model is quite accurate.

\end{itemize}
